One vote per family

Max Howell · September 24, 2026

Democracy gives every adult a vote. I wonder if the vote should belong to a family instead—and pass from one generation to the next.

The basic rule would be simple: one vote per family.

You would not gain that vote merely by turning eighteen. You would gain it when you marry and have a child. At that moment, your parents would lose theirs. The franchise would move forward by one generation.

This is not a small adjustment to democracy. It overturns one of its central assumptions: that the individual is the correct unit of political representation. I am proposing a different unit. Not the individual, and not the household as it happens to exist today, but the family as a continuing project across generations.

Why families?

My premise is that people with children have an unavoidable stake in a future they will not live to see. Parents may be selfish, shortsighted, ideological, foolish, or wrong. Having a child does not make anyone wise. But it does alter the horizon on which many people make decisions.

You start asking what the schools will be like in twenty years. Whether your children will be able to afford a home. Whether the economy will give them useful work. Whether the country will be safe, solvent, free, and capable. Policies stop being abstractions. Their consequences will be inherited by people you love more than yourself.

Put bluntly, I suspect single people are more likely to vote hedonistically. They can absolutely care about the future, and many do. Parents can absolutely vote for immediate rewards, and many do. I am making a claim about incentives, not moral worth: a person without children has less personally at stake in the world after them. A family has skin in a future its current members may never see.

A democracy organized around families might therefore be more inclined toward prosperity: economic growth, good education, abundant housing, sound public finances, technological progress, public safety, and institutions designed to survive us. These are not exclusively “family policies.” They are the foundations on which a better future can be built.

A generational franchise

The part I find most interesting is not actually the restriction. It is the transfer.

Today a parent and an adult child each acquire a vote by reaching a certain age. Under this system, a family line would hold a vote and decide, through its own evolution, when that vote passes to the next generation.

If you think your parents are voting for policies that are backward, too conservative, or simply no longer suited to the world, you would have a direct remedy: marry, have children, and take the vote from them.

That is deliberately provocative, but the incentive matters. Every generation complains that the one before it refuses to relinquish power. This system would create a peaceful and personal mechanism for succession. Political control would move to people actively building the next generation rather than remaining indefinitely with people whose principal stake is in preserving the present.

The vote would still be influenced by more than the two people who formally cast it. Grandparents would have experience and perspective their children may lack. They would naturally want some influence over the family’s vote after it passed on. That gives them an additional reason to maintain good relations with their children and grandchildren. Authority would transfer; influence would have to be earned.

It also creates an unapologetically pro-family incentive for younger people. Society says constantly that it needs more children, then builds its institutions as if forming a family were merely one lifestyle among many. In this system, marriage and parenthood would bring not just private responsibilities but public standing.

The problems I have not solved

The idea is simple only until it touches real life. Then the edge cases become the system.

Defining a family would become an exercise in state power. Does adoption count? It should. What about stepchildren, shared custody, same-sex couples, widows, divorcees, separated spouses, foster parents, infertile couples, parents whose child dies, or people caring for children who are not legally theirs? Does a marriage have to come first? How would the system treat cultures with different family structures? Every definition would create painful boundaries and incentives to arrange intimate lives around government rules.

The generational transfer is mechanically ambiguous. Suppose parents have three adult children. The first has a child, so the parents lose their vote. If the other two later form families, do all three new families receive votes? Presumably yes—but then one vote has become three. Perhaps that is correct because there are now three distinct families. But it means the system is not literally passing a single vote down a family line. It is creating and extinguishing votes according to a family tree, and the exact rules would determine who holds power.

It could reward quantity rather than stewardship. If every new family eventually creates another vote, large family networks gain greater political power over time. That may be a democratic reflection of their investment in the future, or it may turn politics into a fertility contest. A society can need more children without wanting political power allocated by reproductive success.

It would be easy to game and unpleasant to police. Government would need to verify marriages, births, parentage, custody, divorce, and perhaps whether a family is genuine. Votes would switch on and off after intimate life events. Fraud investigations would invade precisely the relationships the system claims to honor.

Family influence can become family domination. I imagine grandparents staying close to their children because they want to retain influence. The darker version is financial pressure, inheritance threats, emotional blackmail, or family blocs controlled by a patriarch or matriarch. A system built to strengthen intergenerational bonds might instead make those bonds transactional.

What the thought experiment exposes

I do not have clean answers to those objections. Some may be implementation problems. Some may be fatal.

But I think the thought experiment reveals a genuine weakness in the system we have. Democracy gives equal weight to radically different stakes in the future. It does little to reward long-term thinking, and it allows one generation to impose costs on the next without giving that next generation a voice.

One vote per family is an attempt to make political representation continuous across time. The family receives the vote because the family is the institution through which the future becomes personal. The vote passes because no generation should own the future forever.

Perhaps this exact system cannot survive its contradictions. At minimum, though, it asks a question our politics avoids: if democracy is meant to build a future, should political power belong equally to people who have made profoundly different investments in it?
